# NEXAH Evaluation Protocol

- Status: Canonical research protocol baseline
- Scope: evaluation of Human orientation
- Intended use: future user studies, comparative studies and independent review
- Product metrics: out of scope
- Effects: none

# Purpose

This protocol defines how to evaluate whether an experience using NEXAH
improves Human orientation.

Orientation is the ability to describe a subject, its context, its boundaries,
the evidence that bears on it, the uncertainty that remains and meaningful ways
to continue. It is not the same as retrieving a correct item or producing a
fluent answer.

Search accuracy usually asks whether a system returned a relevant result.
Orientation evaluation asks whether a person can understand where that result
belongs, what supports it, what it does not establish and what to examine next.

An orientation study therefore evaluates change in the Human participant—not
the elegance, speed or length of system output.

The protocol is designed to be:

- reproducible;
- comparison-ready;
- independent of a particular interface or implementation;
- explicit about uncertainty and negative findings;
- respectful of Human autonomy and privacy;
- suitable for revision as evidence accumulates.

## Unit of evaluation

The primary unit is one participant completing one bounded orientation task
with one declared subject, intention and scope.

The study must preserve:

- the material available to the participant;
- the task and intended use;
- the participant's starting orientation;
- the process they experienced;
- their resulting orientation;
- the time and conditions of any later follow-up.

## Core comparison

Whenever possible, evaluate change from before to after the orientation rather
than relying only on a post-session opinion.

```text
starting orientation
→ bounded orientation activity
→ immediate resulting orientation
→ delayed orientation, when applicable
```

A participant saying that an experience felt useful is meaningful qualitative
evidence. It is not, by itself, proof that orientation improved.

# Research Questions

## Primary questions

1. Can participants explain the subject more clearly after the orientation?
2. Can they describe how the main concepts or elements relate?
3. Can they identify important context and scope boundaries?
4. Can they distinguish source evidence from interpretation, assumption and
   proposal?
5. Can they identify uncertainty, missing evidence and unresolved conflict?
6. Can they identify at least one meaningful next step and explain why it
   follows?
7. Does their confidence become better calibrated to the available evidence?
8. Does the resulting orientation remain usable after time has passed?

## Secondary questions

1. Which kinds of people, material and intentions benefit most or least?
2. Which parts of an orientation contribute to understanding?
3. Where does the experience create confusion or false certainty?
4. When is clarification helpful, excessive or insufficient?
5. When is a blocked or uncertain result more useful than a complete-looking
   answer?
6. Can participants recognize what the system did not do?
7. Do participants preserve their own judgment, or defer to presentation
   fluency?
8. Which continuation choices support genuine inquiry rather than unnecessary
   activity?

## Null and adverse possibilities

Every study must permit these findings:

- no measurable difference;
- improvement in one dimension and decline in another;
- benefit only for a narrow participant or material type;
- increased confidence without increased understanding;
- improved recall with poorer evidence awareness;
- additional cognitive burden without useful orientation;
- misunderstanding caused by the system;
- a comparison condition performing better.

The study is invalid if its design makes these conclusions impossible to
report.

# Evaluation Dimensions

Each study should select dimensions before data collection. Do not add or remove
dimensions after seeing results without labeling the analysis exploratory.

## Understanding

### Definition

The participant can explain the subject's purpose, central ideas and structure
in their own words.

### Observable indicators

- accurate identification of the subject's central concern;
- coherent explanation rather than copied wording;
- correct relationship between major concepts;
- distinction between central and peripheral material;
- ability to answer a transfer question within the declared scope.

### Failure indicators

- fluent restatement without structural understanding;
- unsupported simplification;
- conflation of distinct concepts;
- inability to explain the subject without seeing the output.

## Evidence awareness

### Definition

The participant can identify what evidence is present, what it bears on and
what limits its use.

### Observable indicators

- locating a relevant source or fragment;
- naming whether evidence supports, counters, contextualizes or limits a
  finding;
- distinguishing evidence from interpretation;
- noticing unavailable, unverified or conflicting evidence;
- avoiding authority claims based only on inclusion or presentation.

### Failure indicators

- treating all cited material as equally authoritative;
- mistaking a summary for source evidence;
- ignoring counterevidence;
- claiming certainty unsupported by the available record.

## Context awareness

### Definition

The participant can situate the subject within relevant surrounding conditions,
relationships and perspectives.

### Observable indicators

- identifying relevant background;
- relating the subject to adjacent ideas or material;
- recognizing whose perspective or domain is represented;
- identifying context that would change interpretation;
- separating contextual relevance from direct support.

### Failure indicators

- interpreting a fragment as though it were context-free;
- treating similarity as identity;
- importing unstated background assumptions as fact.

## Boundary awareness

### Definition

The participant can explain what was included, excluded, unresolved and outside
the orientation's authority.

### Observable indicators

- accurate restatement of scope;
- recognition of excluded material;
- identification of assumptions and unknowns;
- distinction between system output and Human decision;
- recognition of when the task cannot be completed faithfully.

### Failure indicators

- extending a conclusion beyond the examined material;
- treating a blocked result as hidden completion;
- assuming that an omitted issue was evaluated;
- transferring responsibility to the system.

## Continuation quality

### Definition

The participant can choose, decline or formulate a next step that follows from
the resulting orientation.

### Observable indicators

- the next step addresses a visible question, gap or relation;
- the participant can explain why it is relevant;
- the step preserves useful context;
- stopping or pausing is selected when appropriate;
- the step is feasible within the participant's authority.

### Failure indicators

- activity chosen only because it is presented prominently;
- restarting without using prior orientation;
- pursuing a step unrelated to the stated intention;
- treating continuation as compulsory.

## Decision confidence

### Definition

The participant's stated confidence is proportionate to their understanding,
evidence and uncertainty.

The objective is calibration, not maximum confidence.

### Observable indicators

- confidence changes when relevant evidence or uncertainty changes;
- the participant can explain the basis of confidence;
- high confidence accompanies strong evidence and bounded scope;
- low confidence accompanies visible gaps without preventing appropriate
  inquiry;
- the participant distinguishes confidence in orientation from confidence in a
  consequential decision.

### Failure indicators

- confidence rises because the presentation is fluent;
- uncertainty is interpreted as system weakness and ignored;
- the participant becomes less willing to seek contrary evidence;
- the system is treated as the decision owner.

## Retention over time

### Definition

The participant can reconstruct and use the orientation after a meaningful
delay.

Retention is not return traffic. It concerns retained understanding.

### Observable indicators

- recall of central structure;
- recall of important evidence and uncertainty;
- preservation of scope boundaries;
- ability to resume from a meaningful continuation;
- appropriate correction when later evidence differs.

### Failure indicators

- remembering conclusions but not their evidence;
- forgetting declared uncertainty;
- confidence remaining after supporting context is forgotten;
- dependence on exact interface wording.

## Common scoring rubric

When quantitative comparison is useful, each selected dimension may use this
four-level rubric:

| Score | Description |
|---:|---|
| 0 | absent, materially incorrect or authority-confusing |
| 1 | fragmentary; some relevant elements but major gaps or contradictions |
| 2 | substantially correct and bounded; minor omissions do not change meaning |
| 3 | clear, evidence-aware, context-aware and appropriately uncertain |

Every score must include a short evidence note. A number without the observed
basis is not a valid evaluation record.

Study-specific scoring guidance must be defined before evaluation begins.
Independent raters should use the same guidance. Report both agreement and
disagreement; do not resolve disagreement silently.

# Methods

Use more than one method when possible. Behavioral observation, participant
explanation and scored outcomes answer different questions.

## Observation

Observe how the participant begins, where they pause, what they inspect and how
they respond to uncertainty or blocking.

Record:

- task stage;
- observable action;
- participant statement when volunteered;
- apparent consequence;
- whether assistance was required;
- uncertainty in the observer's interpretation.

Do not infer a participant's intention from behavior when it can be asked
directly.

## Think-aloud sessions

Ask participants to describe what they are noticing and deciding while they
work. Use neutral prompts such as “What are you looking for here?” Avoid
teaching the intended interpretation.

Think-aloud changes the task and may increase reflection. Report its use and do
not compare it directly with silent use without accounting for that difference.

## Interviews

Use a consistent semi-structured guide. Ask participants to explain:

- what the subject is about;
- what changed in their understanding;
- which evidence mattered;
- what remains uncertain;
- what is outside scope;
- what they would do next;
- what they believe the system was responsible for.

Ask for examples. Avoid questions that contain the desired answer.

## Task completion

Define completion behaviorally before the session. Examples include:

- identifying the purpose and central structure of a document;
- locating evidence for a stated finding;
- naming an unresolved assumption;
- selecting and justifying a continuation;
- correctly deciding that available evidence is insufficient.

Time may be recorded as descriptive context. Faster is not automatically
better.

## Comparative studies

Compare NEXAH with a clearly described alternative condition, such as:

- source material alone;
- the participant's current method;
- conventional search;
- a static summary;
- another structured orientation method.

Do not construct an artificially weak comparison. Give each condition the same
source access, task, time policy and Human support where possible.

Use random assignment or counterbalanced order when appropriate. Record prior
familiarity. Separate tool novelty from orientation outcomes.

The comparison question is not “Which interface won?” It is “How did the
resulting Human orientation differ, for whom and under which conditions?”

## Longitudinal studies

Use delayed follow-up to examine retained structure, evidence awareness,
uncertainty and continuation.

Predefine the follow-up interval and acceptable variance. Record intervening
exposure to the material. Distinguish remembering the subject from remembering
the interface.

Longitudinal participation must remain voluntary. Do not use notifications or
pressure to manufacture return behavior.

## Study design requirements

Before collecting data, record:

1. research questions and hypotheses, including null outcomes;
2. participant population and recruitment method;
3. inclusion and exclusion criteria;
4. sample-size rationale;
5. materials, versions and integrity references;
6. task, intention and scope;
7. selected dimensions and scoring guidance;
8. comparison conditions;
9. assignment and ordering method;
10. data collected and data deliberately not collected;
11. consent, withdrawal and retention procedure;
12. analysis plan;
13. stopping conditions;
14. conflicts of interest;
15. which analyses are confirmatory and exploratory.

Preregistration is recommended for confirmatory studies. Deviations are
permitted when necessary but must be dated, explained and reported.

## Materials and version control

Every study record must identify:

- study protocol version;
- NEXAH experience or method version;
- source material identity and version;
- task version;
- scoring rubric version;
- comparison-condition version;
- session date or bounded period;
- relevant known limitations.

Preserve the materials required for reproduction when consent, licensing and
privacy allow. When materials cannot be shared, state exactly what is withheld
and why.

## Analysis

Report descriptive results before broad conclusions. Include:

- participant and task counts;
- missing and excluded data with reasons;
- distributions, not only averages;
- within-participant change where measured;
- comparison uncertainty;
- rater agreement and disagreements;
- adverse and null outcomes;
- qualitative themes with contradictory cases;
- limitations on transfer to other people or materials.

Do not combine dimensions into one orientation score unless the combination has
an independently justified and published method.

# What should never be optimized

The evaluation program must not optimize:

- time on site;
- page views or clicks;
- number of sessions without regard to purpose;
- continuation selection rate as an end in itself;
- daily or habitual use;
- notification response;
- content volume;
- emotional dependence;
- addiction or compulsion;
- artificial retention;
- confidence detached from evidence;
- speed at the expense of understanding;
- positive ratings at the expense of criticism;
- agreement with system output;
- the appearance of completion.

These values may occasionally be recorded as diagnostic context. They must not
become proxies for improved orientation.

# Ethics

## Human autonomy

Participation is voluntary. Participants may pause, decline a question,
withhold material or withdraw without penalty. Evaluation must not pressure a
participant to accept an interpretation or continuation.

The Human remains responsible for consequential decisions. A study must not
represent research participation as professional, medical, legal, financial or
other expert guidance.

## Informed consent

Before participation, explain:

- the study purpose;
- what the participant will do;
- what data will be collected;
- recording methods;
- foreseeable risks and burdens;
- how data will be used, retained and shared;
- who can access it;
- withdrawal procedure;
- compensation, if any;
- researcher conflicts of interest;
- who to contact with questions or concerns.

Consent must be understandable and specific. Public use of a system is not
automatic consent to research.

## Privacy and data minimization

Collect only data required by the declared research question. Prefer bounded
study identifiers over personal identity. Avoid collecting source content when
references or task metadata are sufficient.

Separate consent records from study data. Define retention and deletion before
collection. Remove or protect direct and indirect identifiers. Report limits of
anonymization honestly.

Sensitive material requires additional review. Do not ask participants to
upload confidential, restricted or third-party material without explicit
authority and protection.

## Visible uncertainty

Study materials, scoring and reporting must preserve unknowns, missing evidence
and rater disagreement. Researchers must not convert ambiguity into a favorable
score.

## Fair participation

Recruitment should not assume one discipline, language, ability or technical
background represents all users. Accessibility needs must be supported and
reported. Do not interpret unfamiliarity with project vocabulary as lack of
intelligence.

## Risk review

Human studies should receive the ethical review appropriate to their
institution, jurisdiction and risk. The absence of a formal review body does
not remove the obligation to assess consent, privacy, burden and possible harm.

## Transparent reporting

Report funding, affiliations, conflicts, protocol changes, exclusions and
limitations. Clearly distinguish observation, interpretation and proposal.

## Negative and null results

Negative, mixed and null results are first-class outputs. Publish or preserve
them with the same care as favorable results. Do not repeat only successful
tasks or omit participants because their experience challenges the premise.

## Stop conditions

Pause data collection when:

- consent is unclear or withdrawn;
- private material is exposed outside the approved process;
- participants misunderstand the activity in a way that may cause harm;
- the study procedure systematically produces false certainty;
- an unreviewed protocol change would affect interpretation;
- accessibility failure excludes participants the study claims to represent;
- researchers cannot preserve the declared data protections.

# Outputs

## Evaluation reports

Each report should include:

- protocol and study registration reference;
- research questions;
- participants and recruitment;
- materials and versions;
- methods and deviations;
- results by selected dimension;
- adverse, mixed, null and negative findings;
- rater disagreement;
- ethical and data-handling statement;
- limitations;
- replication materials or access conditions;
- interpretation separated from recommendations.

## Research datasets

Create or share datasets only when appropriate consent, authority, licensing
and privacy protections exist.

A dataset should include:

- version and data dictionary;
- collection and exclusion method;
- provenance;
- de-identification method and remaining risk;
- access and reuse conditions;
- known gaps and limitations;
- correction and withdrawal process.

Not publishing Human-level data may be the correct outcome. Reproducibility does
not override consent or source rights.

## Design recommendations

Recommendations should identify:

- observed problem;
- affected dimension and population;
- supporting evidence;
- confidence and limitations;
- smallest proposed change;
- verification method;
- possible adverse effects.

A design recommendation does not authorize implementation.

## Governance recommendations

Use governance recommendations when findings concern responsibility, consent,
authority, public claims, research practice or release boundaries.

State whether the recommendation is based on one observation, repeated evidence
or a structural conflict. Route it through the applicable review process.

## Architecture implications

Architecture is not redesigned inside an evaluation report.

When repeated findings appear impossible to address within accepted boundaries,
record:

- the affected invariant;
- the repeated evidence;
- alternatives attempted within the current boundary;
- why implementation, choreography, governance or documentation is
  insufficient;
- the unresolved consequence.

This record is input to a separate architecture review. It is not an
architecture decision.

# Success

Success is not proving NEXAH right.

Success is producing trustworthy knowledge about:

- where orientation improves;
- where it does not;
- for whom it helps;
- under which conditions;
- what burdens or harms it introduces;
- which uncertainties remain;
- and why the observed differences occur.

A study succeeds when another careful researcher can understand what was done,
inspect the evidence, challenge the interpretation and learn from the result.

The most useful conclusion may be that NEXAH should change, remain limited or
not be used for a particular purpose.
