# Orientation Cases

Orientation Cases are the primary public experience of NEXAH.

A case begins with something a person wants to understand. It does not begin
with an explanation of NEXAH.

Every subject can become an Orientation Case:

- a scientific theory;
- a historical event;
- a mathematical idea;
- a technology;
- an engineering problem;
- a medical question;
- a philosophical argument;
- a work of art;
- a map;
- a language;
- a piece of music;
- a natural process;
- a question from everyday life.

Orientation is independent of subject. The sources, methods and uncertainties
change from one domain to another. The need to understand position, context,
evidence, boundaries and possible next steps does not.

## Why cases matter

People rarely begin with:

> I want to understand NEXAH.

They begin with:

> I want to understand something.

They may want to understand plate tectonics, a proof, a revolution, a medical
paper, a programming language, a building, a poem or a place on a map.

The case is therefore the natural entry point.

The visitor first experiences Orientation. They see a subject placed in
context. They follow important relationships. They inspect evidence. They
notice assumptions and uncertainty. They see where the present account ends
and where inquiry could continue.

Only afterwards do they need to discover that this way of finding their
bearings is called NEXAH.

Cases make the idea concrete before asking anyone to learn its name.

## Canonical Orientation Case structure

Every Orientation Case follows exactly the same public structure and order:

```markdown
# [Title]

# Human Intention

# Starting Orientation

# Subject

# Context

# Relationships

# Evidence

# Assumptions

# Uncertainty

# Boundaries

# Unknowns

# Continuations

# Reflection
```

The shared structure is not a stylistic template. It is what makes cases
comparable, inspectable and learnable across domains.

Writers may use tables, diagrams, lists and subsections inside these sections.
They must not remove a section because it is inconvenient. When a section has
no known content, the case should say so explicitly.

### Title

Name the subject plainly.

The title should help a visitor recognize what they will orient around. Avoid
claims, slogans and conclusions in the title.

Good titles are usually the name of the subject:

- Plate Tectonics
- The French Revolution
- CRISPR Gene Editing
- The Pythagorean Theorem

### Human Intention

State the question or need that brought the person here.

The intention should be written in ordinary Human language. It defines the
direction of the case without assuming the answer.

Examples:

> I want to understand what this is and where I should continue.

> I want to understand why this event happened and which explanations are
> disputed.

> I want to understand what this method can and cannot establish.

The case must preserve this intention. It should not quietly answer a broader
or more convenient question.

### Starting Orientation

Describe a plausible place from which someone might begin.

Include:

- what a newcomer may already know;
- which parts may be unclear;
- common misconceptions that materially affect understanding;
- the first relationships that need to become visible.

Do not describe every reader as uninformed. A starting orientation is an entry
point, not a judgment about the person.

### Subject

Define the topic clearly and within the case's intended depth.

State what kind of thing it is. Distinguish it from nearby concepts when that
distinction matters. Use necessary domain terms, but explain them before relying
on them.

This section answers:

> What are we orienting around?

### Context

Show where the subject belongs.

Relevant context may include:

- historical period;
- scientific field;
- cultural setting;
- technical system;
- geographical region;
- intellectual tradition;
- practical use;
- surrounding debate.

Context is not background added for completeness. Include what changes how the
subject should be understood.

### Relationships

Show what connects to what.

Relationships may express:

- sequence;
- cause and consequence;
- part and whole;
- similarity and difference;
- dependency;
- conflict;
- influence;
- transformation;
- scale;
- spatial position;
- evidence and claim.

Use the smallest useful representation. A short table may clarify a comparison.
A sequence may clarify change over time. A map may clarify spatial relations.
Prose is enough when the relationship is simple.

Do not turn resemblance into identity or correlation into cause.

### Evidence

Separate evidence from explanation.

Every substantive claim should remain inspectable through a real source,
observation, dataset, document, object or declared derivation.

For each source, make visible where applicable:

- title or identity;
- author, publisher or responsible authority;
- version, date or edition;
- exact passage, fragment, record or region;
- public link or access condition;
- what the source bears on;
- whether it supports, counters, contextualizes or limits;
- relevant provenance;
- known limitations.

A long source list is not automatically strong evidence. Prefer a small set of
sources whose role can be explained.

If evidence is missing, unavailable, disputed or unverified, state that. Do not
replace it with fluent explanation.

### Assumptions

Declare the simplifying assumptions that make the case possible.

Examples include:

- introductory rather than specialist depth;
- one accepted definition among several;
- a bounded period or region;
- a simplified model;
- reliance on a particular source edition;
- exclusion of a related debate;
- an assumed level of prior knowledge.

Assumptions are not defects. Hidden assumptions are.

### Uncertainty

Keep uncertainty visible.

Distinguish uncertainty caused by:

- incomplete evidence;
- conflicting evidence;
- measurement limits;
- interpretation;
- changing knowledge;
- unclear identity or version;
- genuine open questions;
- the chosen simplification.

Do not use one general confidence label when the uncertainties are different.
Explain what is uncertain and why it matters.

### Boundaries

State where the case ends.

This section should answer both:

- What is outside scope?
- What cannot be concluded from what is present?

A case about a scientific framework may not support a prediction. A case about
a historical event may not establish one cause. A case about a medical study
may not support advice for an individual. A case about a technology may not
establish that it should be adopted.

Boundaries protect the reader from conclusions the case has not earned.

### Unknowns

List the important questions that remain open after the case.

Unknowns may belong to the subject, the evidence or the reader's next need.
They should be concrete enough to investigate.

Do not include open questions merely to make a case appear profound. Include
the questions that genuinely limit or direct the present orientation.

### Continuations

Offer several meaningful ways to continue.

Each continuation must explain:

1. what the reader could examine next;
2. why that step follows from the current orientation;
3. what context would remain relevant;
4. what new material, evidence or decision may be required.

Useful continuations may:

- follow one concept more deeply;
- inspect a source or dataset;
- compare another perspective;
- test a relationship;
- narrow or expand scope;
- examine counterevidence;
- move from general account to a real case;
- pause because the present orientation is sufficient.

More activity is not automatically better. Stopping is a valid continuation
decision.

### Reflection

Describe how the reader's orientation may have changed.

Return to the starting orientation and make the difference visible:

- Which relationships can now be explained?
- Which evidence can now be inspected?
- Which misconception has been corrected or bounded?
- Which uncertainty is clearer?
- Which next step is now meaningful?

Reflection is not a victory statement. It should also identify what the case
did not change.

## Case categories

Categories help visitors enter the Orientation Library. They do not change the
case structure.

- Science
- Mathematics
- Technology
- Engineering
- History
- Biology
- Medicine
- Geography
- Astronomy
- Physics
- Chemistry
- Earth Science
- Computer Science
- Artificial Intelligence
- Economics
- Philosophy
- Language
- Art
- Music
- Design
- Architecture
- Ecology
- Society

A case may belong to more than one category. Categories are navigation aids,
not claims that knowledge fits into isolated boxes.

Cross-domain cases should preserve the evidence standards and uncertainty of
each participating domain. One domain's authority does not automatically carry
into another.

## Case quality

A publishable case should be:

- **bounded** — the intention and scope are clear;
- **structured** — important relationships are visible;
- **inspectable** — substantive claims lead to identifiable evidence;
- **honest** — assumptions, uncertainty and missing evidence remain visible;
- **balanced** — countering and limiting evidence is not hidden;
- **useful** — continuations follow from the orientation;
- **readable** — necessary language is explained without flattening meaning;
- **faithful** — the case does not claim authority it does not have;
- **reproducible** — another reviewer can inspect the same source versions;
- **revisable** — later correction can preserve the history of what changed.

A case is not improved merely by becoming longer. The preferred case is the
smallest one that creates a trustworthy orientation.

## Website vision

The future Orientation Library is not organized around hundreds of isolated
articles.

It contains hundreds of Orientation Cases.

Every case uses the same public language:

```text
Human Intention
→ Starting Orientation
→ Subject and Context
→ Relationships and Evidence
→ Assumptions, Uncertainty and Boundaries
→ Unknowns and Continuations
→ Reflection
```

A visitor may begin with astronomy and later open a case in music, history or
engineering. The subject changes. The orientation pattern remains familiar.

Over time, visitors learn how Orientation works by experiencing it:

- begin with an intention;
- look for relationships;
- inspect evidence;
- preserve uncertainty;
- respect boundaries;
- choose where to continue.

The cases can serve several purposes without becoming different kinds of
objects:

- public examples;
- teaching material;
- evaluation material;
- community contributions;
- research material;
- starting points for deeper orientation.

The website should make cases easy to explore by subject, relationship,
evidence type and possible continuation while preserving the same readable
case structure.

## Contribution model

Eventually, contributors should be able to propose new Orientation Cases.

A contribution begins with a bounded subject and Human intention. It should
include the complete canonical structure, real sources and an explicit account
of what remains uncertain.

Case review evaluates more than writing style.

### Clarity

- Is the intention understandable?
- Is the subject defined without unnecessary terminology?
- Are the central relationships visible?
- Can a newcomer follow the case without private explanation?

### Evidence

- Can substantive claims be inspected?
- Are identity, version and authority visible where relevant?
- Is evidence separated from explanation?
- Are counterevidence and limitations preserved?

### Boundaries

- Is the scope explicit?
- Does the case avoid conclusions its evidence cannot support?
- Are domain and authority limits visible?

### Uncertainty

- Are unknowns and disagreements stated precisely?
- Does the case avoid invented certainty?
- Are simplifying assumptions declared?

### Continuation quality

- Does each next step follow from a visible element of the case?
- Does it explain why it matters?
- Can the reader also pause or stop?

### Review outcome

A reviewer may:

- accept the case;
- request a bounded correction;
- request stronger or clearer evidence;
- narrow the case;
- preserve a disagreement;
- reject a claim while retaining useful source material;
- defer publication when authority or evidence cannot be established.

Publication does not mean that a case is final. Corrections should remain
traceable, and significant changes should preserve earlier versions rather than
silently rewriting them.

## Relationship to the first example

The existing
[`Plate Tectonics orientation`](../examples/orientation/README.md) demonstrates
the first complete movement from Human intention to evidence, boundaries and
continuation.

Future published Orientation Cases should use the exact canonical order defined
in this document. Existing demonstrations may be brought into that order
through a separate editorial change without altering their evidence or meaning.

## Long-term vision

NEXAH should not become the world's biggest knowledge base.

Information already exists in books, papers, repositories, datasets, archives,
maps, works of art, recordings and Human experience. The Orientation Library
should help people find their bearings across those sources without pretending
to replace them.

Its value should come from the quality of its cases:

- the care of their questions;
- the clarity of their relationships;
- the inspectability of their evidence;
- the honesty of their uncertainty;
- the strength of their boundaries;
- the usefulness of their continuations.

NEXAH should become one of the world's most useful Orientation Libraries.
