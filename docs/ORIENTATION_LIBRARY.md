# The Orientation Library

The Orientation Library is the future public home of Orientation Cases.

It is not a collection of articles arranged on shelves. It is a navigable
landscape in which subjects, questions, evidence, patterns and unknowns remain
connected.

Its first question is not:

> Which category are you in?

It is:

> What do you want to understand?

## Purpose

The Library helps people enter, inspect and continue an orientation.

A visitor may arrive with a clear question, a broad curiosity or a subject they
cannot yet place. The Library should help them find a useful starting point
without requiring them to understand NEXAH first.

Every public item at the center of the Library is an Orientation Case following
the canonical structure in
[`ORIENTATION_CASES.md`](ORIENTATION_CASES.md).

Cases remain bounded and readable on their own. The Library adds a second form
of value: it makes the relationships between cases visible.

## A landscape, not a list

Ordinary collections often begin with a hierarchy:

```text
Science
History
Technology
Medicine
Architecture
Music
Mathematics
```

Those subjects remain useful ways to browse. They are not the only ways.

A person may want to follow:

```text
Evidence Type
Uncertainty
Scale
Pattern
Transformation
Conflict
Systems
Maps
Networks
Time
Geometry
Language
Energy
```

Plate tectonics may begin in Earth Science, then lead through maps, deep time,
energy, evidence from measurement, system boundaries and patterns of change.
A case about musical harmony may connect to Mathematics through ratios, to
Physics through frequency and to History through tuning systems.

The Library should preserve these bridges instead of forcing each case into one
box.

## The visitor's question

Navigation begins with intention.

A visitor should always be able to state, select or refine what they want to
understand. Examples include:

- What is this?
- Why did this happen?
- How do these ideas relate?
- What evidence supports this claim?
- Where do these perspectives disagree?
- What changes across scale?
- What remains unknown?
- What should I understand first?
- Where could I continue?

The Library may offer routes, filters and connections. It must not silently
replace the visitor's question with a category or a popular topic.

When a visitor changes direction, the change should be visible. Exploration is
allowed to evolve; intention should not disappear.

## Entry points

Every entry point is a different view of the same Orientation Cases. None is a
separate knowledge authority.

### Browse by Subject

Begin with a recognizable field or topic such as Science, History, Medicine,
Music or Mathematics.

Subject browsing is useful when the visitor already knows the broad area. Cases
may appear in several subjects when the overlap is genuine.

### Browse by Question

Begin with a Human intention or an open question.

Questions should lead to cases that actually address them, cases that supply
needed context and cases that explain why the question cannot yet be answered.

A question is not merely a keyword. Its direction and scope matter.

### Browse by Relationship

Begin with a kind of connection:

- cause and consequence;
- part and whole;
- similarity and difference;
- dependency;
- influence;
- contradiction;
- sequence;
- transformation;
- spatial relation;
- evidence and claim.

Relationship browsing helps a visitor notice that the same structure can appear
in different domains. Shared structure does not make the subjects identical.

### Browse by Evidence

Begin with the kind of material through which a subject can be inspected:

- direct observation;
- experiment;
- measurement;
- dataset;
- document;
- artifact;
- image;
- map;
- testimony;
- mathematical derivation;
- simulation;
- historical record.

Evidence browsing should show what each source bears on, along with its
authority, version, access and limitations. A case with more sources is not
automatically better.

### Browse by Pattern

Begin with a recurring form such as cycles, branching, symmetry, feedback,
emergence, convergence, diffusion or concentration.

Patterns create bridges across fields. Every pattern connection should explain
what is shared and where the analogy stops.

### Browse by Map

Begin spatially.

A map may organize cases by physical location, region, movement, distribution
or another explicitly defined space. It should show scale, source and
uncertainty. A point or boundary on a map is a representation, not the subject
itself.

### Browse by Timeline

Begin with sequence and change over time.

Timelines may connect events, discoveries, processes, versions or debates.
They should distinguish when something happened from when it was observed,
recorded or interpreted.

### Browse by Concept

Begin with an idea that appears in several cases: evidence, energy, identity,
scale, equilibrium, revolution, language or uncertainty.

Concept pages should orient across cases rather than become substitute
articles. Meaning may differ by discipline; those differences must remain
visible.

### Browse by Difficulty

Begin with the depth and prior knowledge a case expects.

Difficulty describes the demands of a particular case. It does not rank the
importance of the subject or the intelligence of the reader. A case should
explain which terms or prior cases would help.

### Browse by estimated reading time

Begin with the time currently available.

Estimated reading time is descriptive. It must not encourage speed or imply
that finishing a page equals understanding it.

### Browse by Age

Begin with an intended audience age or educational stage when that information
has been reviewed.

Age guidance should concern language, context and safeguarding—not a judgment
of ability. Cases for younger readers still preserve evidence, uncertainty and
boundaries.

### Browse by Discipline

Begin with the practices and evidence standards of a field.

Discipline is more specific than subject. It may help a visitor distinguish,
for example, historical evidence from experimental evidence or mathematical
proof from empirical measurement.

### Browse by cross-disciplinary bridge

Begin where two or more domains meet.

Each bridge should state:

- which cases it connects;
- what relationship is shared;
- which vocabulary changes meaning;
- what evidence belongs to each domain;
- where the bridge is useful;
- where it breaks down.

Cross-disciplinary browsing should reveal differences as carefully as
similarities.

## Connections between cases

Every published Orientation Case should connect explicitly to other cases when
a meaningful connection exists.

Connections are editorial claims. They require a reason. They are not generated
merely because titles share words.

### Related Cases

Cases that share a subject, context, concept or important relationship.

The connection should state what is related. “Related” must not become an
unexplained bucket.

### Contrasting Cases

Cases that expose a meaningful difference, disagreement or opposite structure.

A contrast should identify the comparison lens. Difference does not imply that
one case refutes the other.

### Prerequisite Cases

Cases that establish context or concepts needed to use another case well.

Prerequisites are directional. They should be used sparingly: a visitor may
already have the necessary orientation without reading every earlier case.

### Continuation Cases

Cases that follow naturally from a named unknown, boundary or continuation.

The connection should explain what the next case adds and which context remains
relevant.

### Different Perspectives

Cases that orient around the same subject from distinct declared positions,
methods, communities or disciplines.

Perspectives should not be collapsed into false balance. Their authority,
evidence and scope remain separately inspectable.

### Shared Evidence

Cases that use the same source, dataset, artifact or observation.

Shared evidence does not require shared conclusions. Each case must show how
the evidence relates to its own claims and boundaries.

### Shared Patterns

Cases that exhibit a common structure across subjects.

The connection must identify both the pattern and the limits of the analogy.
Presentation should never turn a suggestive resemblance into evidence of a
causal relationship.

## Connection quality

A useful connection answers four questions:

1. What exactly is connected?
2. What kind of connection is it?
3. Why does it matter for the visitor's orientation?
4. Where does the connection stop?

Connections should be version-aware. When a case changes materially, its
connections should be reviewed rather than assumed to remain valid.

More connections do not make a case more useful. Prefer a few explained paths
over a dense network of unexplained links.

## A visit to the Library

Imagine a visitor arriving with a simple intention:

> I want to understand why earthquakes happen.

They may begin with the
[`Plate Tectonics orientation`](../examples/orientation/README.md). From there,
the Library can make several paths visible:

- a prerequisite path toward Earth's internal structure;
- an evidence path toward GPS measurements;
- a map path toward plate boundaries and earthquake distribution;
- a contrasting path toward earthquakes within plates;
- a continuation path toward regional hazard assessment;
- an uncertainty path toward unresolved details of plate-driving forces;
- a shared-pattern path toward stress accumulation in other systems.

The visitor chooses one path because it follows their intention. The Library
does not require them to finish a category or follow the most popular route.

As they move, their orientation develops:

- familiar context remains visible;
- new cases add relationships and evidence;
- contradictions remain available;
- unknowns become frontiers;
- the visitor can return, change direction or stop.

The living map is not valuable because it moves or looks complex. It is valuable
because it makes these choices and their reasons visible.

## The living map

In the future Library:

- each Orientation Case is a node;
- explicit case connections are paths;
- categories and concepts are views, not containers;
- evidence is inspectable from the cases that use it;
- unknowns and unresolved boundaries form visible frontiers;
- continuations show possible directions;
- maps and timelines reveal spatial and temporal structure;
- the visitor's intention provides the current point of reference.

The map should work at more than one scale.

At the widest view, a visitor may notice bridges between domains. At a closer
view, they may see the evidence and unknowns around one case. Inside a case,
they should always return to the complete readable orientation.

No visualization should be the only way to access the Library. Lists, text,
keyboard navigation, clear headings and accessible relationship descriptions
must provide equivalent routes.

## What remains visible

Wherever a case appears—in a map, list, connection view or search result—the
Library should make enough context visible to prevent a misleading choice.

Useful orientation cues include:

- case title;
- Human intention;
- subject and discipline;
- expected depth;
- estimated reading time;
- evidence availability;
- important uncertainty or boundary;
- the reason for the present connection;
- accessibility or source-access limitations.

These cues help a visitor choose. They do not replace reading the case.

## Unknowns as frontiers

Unknowns are part of the Library, not gaps to hide.

A frontier may show:

- an unanswered question shared by several cases;
- missing or unavailable evidence;
- a conflict between interpretations;
- a boundary between disciplines;
- a topic without a reviewed case;
- a relationship that remains only a proposal.

A frontier should identify why it is open. It must not appear as an empty case
or an implied promise that an answer is coming.

Visitors and contributors may use frontiers to find meaningful work. The
Library should value a well-described unknown as much as a polished case.

## Evidence in the Library

Evidence remains attached to the claims and cases that use it.

The Library may help visitors discover:

- every case using a source;
- cases that use the source differently;
- supporting and countering relationships;
- changes in source version or access;
- provenance and authority;
- cases with visible evidence gaps.

The Library does not turn source frequency into authority. A widely used source
may still be limited, contested or inappropriate for another case.

Evidence views must preserve the distinction between source, explanation,
interpretation and Human judgment.

## Editorial principles

### Prefer depth over quantity

One carefully bounded case is more useful than many shallow pages. Growth should
follow review capacity, not publishing targets.

### Prefer inspectable evidence over completeness

A smaller orientation with sources that can be examined is better than a broad
account whose claims cannot be traced.

### Prefer honest uncertainty over false certainty

Unknown, disputed and unavailable are valid states. They should remain visible
in cases, connections and navigation.

### Prefer meaningful connections over isolated pages

A case should show how it relates to a wider landscape. Connections must be
explained rather than added for volume.

### Prefer Human direction over hidden ranking

The visitor's question, chosen route and declared scope should guide the
experience. Popularity, engagement and novelty must not silently determine what
appears most important.

### Prefer stable meaning over visual novelty

Maps, networks and timelines are useful only when their relationships can be
read, inspected and explained consistently.

### Prefer correction with history over silent replacement

Cases and connections will improve. Material changes should remain traceable so
that earlier orientations can still be understood.

## Editorial growth

The Library should grow through reviewed cases and reviewed connections.

A proposed case is evaluated through the canonical Case blueprint. A proposed
connection is evaluated for type, reason, evidence and boundary. A new category
or browsing lens should demonstrate that it helps visitors orient; it should
not be added merely because it is available as metadata.

The Library may begin very small:

- a few cases from different domains;
- a small number of well-explained connections;
- visible evidence and unknowns;
- several accessible entry points;
- clear paths for correction and contribution.

This is enough to test whether people can explore Orientation without being
overwhelmed by the map.

## Uses of the Library

The same reviewed cases may support:

- first public encounters with NEXAH;
- teaching and facilitated inquiry;
- independent learning;
- evaluation of Human orientation;
- research into relationships and evidence;
- contributor training;
- examples for developers and designers;
- starting points for deeper, personal orientation.

Use in a new setting does not automatically validate that setting. Educational,
research or professional use requires the evidence and review appropriate to
its context.

## Long-term vision

Wikipedia became a library of articles.

GitHub became a library of code.

arXiv became a library of papers.

NEXAH should become a library of Orientation.

Not by containing all information.

Not by replacing the places where knowledge already lives.

By offering a clear, inspectable way to enter a subject, see its relationships,
understand its evidence and limits, and choose where to continue.

The Library succeeds when a visitor leaves with better bearings—not simply
with more pages opened.
